VP vs VNP, and what it has to do with P vs NP

Two formulas differ only by a few minus signs. One is easy to compute. The other looks impossibly hard. Whether it really is hard is the question called VP vs VNP, the algebraic cousin of P vs NP.

The small print

“Cheap” and “quick” have an exact meaning here: the number of steps grows no faster than some fixed power of the size of the grid, like n³. “Hard” means it outgrows every such power, like 2ⁿ.

Clearing rows uses division, and VP only allows adding and multiplying. It is known that the divisions can be taken out at a modest cost, so the determinant is in VP all the same.

The permanent is the hardest thing in VNP in any number system where 1 + 1 is not 0. Where it is 0, plus and minus are the same thing, and the permanent is the determinant.

The link between the two questions is due to Peter Bürgisser. He showed that if VP were equal to VNP, every problem in NP would have small circuits. Over the complex numbers, his proof assumes the generalized Riemann hypothesis. That is why the circuit form of P ≠ NP needs VP ≠ VNP to be true, and why proving VP ≠ VNP does not prove P ≠ NP.

The episode calls the proof of VP ≠ VNP “claimed”, and so does this page. It explains the question and does not check the proof.
